How Yangshuo’s Locals Use Herbs in Daily Life

Home / Travel Blog / Blog Details

If you have ever wandered through the misty karst mountains of Yangshuo, you know that the landscape is not just a postcard. It is a living pharmacy. The locals here do not just admire the greenery—they eat it, drink it, bathe in it, and heal with it. For travelers who want to go beyond the typical bamboo raft ride and beer fish dinner, understanding how Yangshuo’s locals use herbs in daily life is the ultimate cultural immersion. This is not a museum exhibit. This is how people wake up, cook, treat a cold, and even scent their homes. And trust me, once you see it, you will never look at a clump of roadside weeds the same way again.

The Morning Cup: Herbal Tea as a Daily Ritual

In Yangshuo, the day does not start with coffee. It starts with a steaming cup of herbal tea. Walk into any local home or small eatery before 8 a.m., and you will see a thermos or a clay pot brewing something that looks like murky pond water but smells like a forest after rain.

The Ubiquitous “Liang Cha” (Cooling Tea)

The most common morning brew is liang cha, or cooling tea. It is not one herb but a blend. The base is usually jin yin hua (honeysuckle), ju hua (chrysanthemum), and gan cao (licorice root). Locals drink it year-round, but especially in the humid summer months. The theory is simple: Yangshuo’s subtropical climate traps heat and dampness in the body. Liang cha flushes it out.

I once asked a 70-year-old grandmother in a village near Moon Hill why she drinks it even in winter. She laughed and said in broken English, “Winter also have fire inside. Too much hot pot. Need cool.” She pointed to her throat and made a soothing gesture. That is the kind of everyday logic that travelers rarely see in guidebooks.

Wild Mint and Luo Han Guo

Another morning favorite is a tea made from wild mint (bo he) and luo han guo (monk fruit). Monk fruit is a small, round, brown fruit that tastes intensely sweet but has zero calories. Locals crush it and add fresh mint leaves from the garden. The result is a refreshing, naturally sweet drink that clears the sinuses and wakes up the digestion. Many guesthouses in Yangshuo now offer this as a welcome drink. If you stay at a family-run inn, do not be surprised if the owner hands you a cup the moment you check in. It is not just hospitality—it is preventive medicine.

Cooking with Herbs: Beyond the Tourist Menu

Every traveler to Yangshuo has tried Beer Fish (pi jiu yu). It is delicious. But the real magic of local cuisine lies in the herbs that never make it onto the English menu. Locals do not just use salt and soy sauce. They use leaves, roots, and stems that most Westerners would call weeds.

Perilla and Purple Basil in Stir-Fries

Walk through the morning market in Yangshuo town, and you will see piles of zi su (perilla) and jiu ceng ta (Thai basil or “nine-story tower”). These are not garnishes. They are core ingredients. Locals tear perilla leaves into stir-fried pork or frog legs. The herb has a slightly bitter, anise-like flavor that cuts through the richness of the meat. It also has anti-inflammatory properties, which matters in a cuisine that loves deep-frying and chili oil.

I watched a street vendor near West Street make a simple stir-fry of snails with perilla and pickled chilies. She told me, “Without zi su, the snail tastes like mud. With zi su, it tastes like Yangshuo.” She was not wrong. The herb literally transforms the dish.

Mugwort in Glutinous Rice Cakes

If you visit Yangshuo in early spring, you will encounter a bright green snack called ai cao ba (mugwort rice cake). Locals pick fresh mugwort leaves (ai cao), blanch them, and mash them into glutinous rice flour. The result is a chewy, earthy cake often stuffed with red bean paste or peanuts. Mugwort is not just for flavor. In traditional belief, it warms the body and drives away “cold dampness.” After a rainy spring day in the karst hills, eating one of these cakes feels like putting a blanket on your insides.

I tried one at a village near the Li River. The woman who made it told me her grandmother taught her the recipe. She said, “We eat this when the weather changes. It keeps the stomach happy.” For travelers prone to digestive issues from unfamiliar food, this is a gentle, local remedy disguised as a dessert.

Herbal Baths: Washing Away the Travel Fatigue

After a long day of cycling through rice paddies or hiking to the top of Xianggong Mountain, your muscles will scream. Locals have a solution: herbal baths. This is not a spa treatment for tourists (though some places now offer it). This is a home practice.

Artemisia and Ginger Foot Soaks

Many rural homes have a dedicated wooden bucket for foot soaks. The standard recipe includes dried artemisia (ai ye), ginger slices, and sometimes hong hua (safflower). The herbs are boiled in water until the liquid turns dark brown. You soak your feet for 20 minutes. The artemisia has antimicrobial properties, the ginger stimulates circulation, and the safflower is believed to move stagnant blood.

I once stayed with a host family in a village near Fuli Bridge. After a day of cycling in the rain, the host mother insisted I soak my feet. She said in Mandarin, “Your feet are cold. That means your whole body is cold. This will fix it.” She was right. I slept like a stone that night. For travelers who are not used to the humidity and sudden temperature drops in Yangshuo, this simple practice can prevent colds and muscle soreness.

Full-Body Herbal Baths for Postpartum and Recovery

In more traditional households, new mothers take a full-body bath with a blend of ai ye, sang ye (mulberry leaf), and jing jie (schizonepeta). This is believed to cleanse the body after childbirth and prevent “wind” from entering the joints. Some guesthouses now offer a lighter version for tourists—a “herbal steam bath” where you sit in a wooden box with only your head sticking out, while a pot of herbs boils beneath you. It is intense but deeply relaxing. If you have a cold or feel run down, this is better than any over-the-counter medicine.

Herbs for First Aid: The Traveler’s Lifesaver

You are hiking in the karst hills. You slip on a wet rock. You get a scrape, a bruise, or an insect bite. Before you reach for your first-aid kit, look around. The locals have been treating these injuries with roadside plants for centuries.

Plantain Leaves for Cuts and Stings

The most common roadside herb is che qian cao (plantain). It grows everywhere—along paths, in cracks in the pavement, near rice fields. Locals pick a leaf, chew it (or crush it with a rock), and apply the pulp directly to a cut or a bee sting. The leaf has natural antibacterial properties and draws out toxins. I saw a farmer do this after a bamboo splinter got lodged in his hand. He chewed the leaf, pressed it on the wound, and wrapped it with a strip of his shirt. Two days later, the wound was clean and healing.

For travelers who are squeamish about chewing leaves, you can crush them with a clean stone and apply the juice. It works just as well.

Artemisia for Mosquito Repellent

Mosquitoes in Yangshuo are relentless, especially near water. Locals do not rely on DEET. They burn dried artemisia (ai cao) like incense. The smoke smells pleasant to humans but repels insects. In some villages, people hang bundles of fresh artemisia near doorways. Others rub crushed leaves on their skin.

I tried this one evening during a homestay near the Yulong River. The host family lit a small bundle of dried artemisia on a ceramic plate and placed it under the dinner table. The smoke drifted up through the table slats. The mosquitoes vanished. It was more effective than any citronella candle I have ever used. Plus, it smelled like a campfire in a herb garden.

Herbs in Home Remedies: What Locals Actually Do When They Get Sick

Tourists get sick in Yangshuo. It happens. The water is different. The food is spicier. The humidity can trigger headaches or sinus issues. When locals get sick, they do not rush to the pharmacy. They go to the herb market or their own backyard.

Ginger and Brown Sugar for the Common Cold

The first sign of a sniffle triggers a specific response: ginger and brown sugar tea (jiang tang shui). Locals slice fresh ginger, boil it with dark brown sugar (which is less refined than white sugar), and drink it hot. The ginger warms the body, induces sweating, and breaks a fever. The sugar provides quick energy. It is the local equivalent of chicken soup.

I caught a mild cold during a rainy week in Yangshuo. My host noticed I was blowing my nose. Without asking, she brought me a mug of this tea. She said, “Drink this. Then sleep. Tomorrow you will be fine.” She was right. By morning, my symptoms had faded. For travelers who do not want to take strong medication, this is a gentle, effective alternative.

Bitter Tea for Stomach Upset

If you eat too much street food or drink the wrong water, locals turn to ku cha (bitter tea). This is a strong brew made from ku ding cha (a type of holly leaf). It is intensely bitter—almost undrinkable at first. But locals swear by it for killing bacteria in the gut and stopping diarrhea. A single cup is usually enough.

I watched a backpacker in a small restaurant near the Li River complain of an upset stomach. The owner, a middle-aged woman, brought him a tiny cup of this dark liquid. He grimaced after one sip. She said, “Good. That means it is working.” Twenty minutes later, he looked noticeably better. He asked for more. She shook her head. “One cup is enough. Too much will make you weak.” That kind of precise, intuitive dosing is what makes local herbal knowledge so impressive.

Herbs in Daily Cleaning and Home Care

Herbs are not just for eating and healing. They are also for cleaning. Locals use them to wash floors, scent rooms, and even treat laundry.

Pomelo Peel and Mugwort for Room Freshening

Instead of chemical air fresheners, locals dry pomelo peels and burn them alongside mugwort. The combination produces a clean, citrusy, slightly smoky scent that neutralizes odors. In some homes, people place bowls of dried orange peel and star anise in corners to absorb dampness and repel insects.

I visited a farmhouse near the Xianggong Mountain viewpoint. The host had hung bundles of dried herbs from the ceiling beams. She said, “This keeps the house dry and the bugs away. Also it smells like my grandmother’s house.” The scent was subtle but unmistakable—earthy, warm, and natural. It made the concrete walls feel like a sanctuary.

Tea Seed Meal for Dishwashing

One of the most surprising uses of herbs is cha fu (tea seed meal). This is the leftover residue from pressing tea seeds for oil. Locals use it as a natural soap. It creates a mild lather, cuts through grease, and is completely biodegradable. Many rural households still use it for washing dishes and even shampooing hair.

I tried it once. The texture is like coarse sand, and it does not foam like modern soap. But it left my hands feeling clean without the dryness that chemical soaps cause. For eco-conscious travelers, this is a fascinating glimpse into zero-waste living that predates the modern sustainability movement.

The Herb Markets: Where Travelers Can Experience This Firsthand

If you want to see all of this in action, head to the Yangshuo morning market (near the bus station) or the larger Fuli Market on market days. You will find stalls piled high with dried roots, leaves, and seeds. The sellers are usually elderly women who have been harvesting these herbs for decades.

What to Look For

  • Dried honeysuckle flowers – yellow and white, used for tea.
  • Monk fruit – brown, wrinkled, about the size of a tennis ball.
  • Artemisia bundles – long, dark green leaves tied with string.
  • Fresh perilla – purple-green leaves sold in handfuls.
  • Dried tangerine peel – aged for years, used in cooking and tea.

Do not be afraid to ask questions. Most sellers are happy to explain the uses, especially if you show genuine curiosity. A simple “Zhe ge zenme yong?” (How do you use this?) will open a conversation. Even if you do not buy anything, the market is a living classroom.

How Travelers Can Incorporate These Practices

You do not need to live in Yangshuo to benefit from these herbal traditions. But while you are there, you can try a few simple things:

  1. Start your day with liang cha instead of coffee. Many guesthouses offer it for free. It will help your digestion adjust to the local food.

  2. Order a dish with perilla. Ask for zi su chao rou (perilla stir-fried with pork) or zi su chao luo si (perilla with snails). It is a flavor you will not forget.

  3. Buy a small bundle of dried artemisia. Light a piece in your hotel room at night to repel mosquitoes. Just be careful with fire—use a ceramic dish.

  4. Try a herbal foot soak. Some massage places in Yangshuo offer this as an add-on. It costs about 30-50 RMB and is worth every yuan after a long hike.

  5. Visit a herb market early in the morning. Go before 9 a.m. when the selection is best. Bring small bills. Do not be afraid to touch and smell.

The Deeper Lesson: Herbs as a Way of Life

What strikes me most about Yangshuo’s relationship with herbs is not the specific recipes or remedies. It is the attitude. Herbs are not exotic or special. They are ordinary. They are part of the daily rhythm, like sweeping the floor or boiling water. There is no separation between food and medicine, between cleaning and healing. A plant that treats a cut is the same plant that flavors a soup.

For travelers coming from cultures where health is managed through pills and clinics, this is a quiet revelation. It suggests that wellness does not have to be expensive or complicated. It can be as simple as knowing what grows at your feet and how to use it.

The next time you are in Yangshuo, skip a few tourist attractions. Spend an hour at the herb market. Drink the bitter tea. Eat the green cake. Soak your feet in brown water. You will leave not just with photos of mountains, but with a small piece of a wisdom that has been growing here for centuries. And that is a souvenir that will not fit in your suitcase—but it will stay with you long after you go home.
